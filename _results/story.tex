\section{Methods}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{RL agent objectives}

The agent's job each step is: \emph{``Pick a point $x$ in the design space. The oracle will tell you the (noisy) value $f(x)$. Now pick another.''} The agent does this 100 times per episode. That is the task.

During reinforcement learning, the agent is not told what is ``right''; instead it gets a scalar reward, $R$, each step and has to figure out an acquisition policy that maximizes total reward.

In this setup the reward is not ``did you accurately map out the objective?'' It is ``did your surrogate model's uncertainty audit improve?'' After every query, we refit a small polynomial surrogate to all the data that has been seen so far. That surrogate gives a predicted mean $\mu(x)$ and a predicted uncertainty $\sigma(x)$ over the whole domain. We then run an \emph{audit metric} on the surrogate. E.g.\ ``is your $\sigma$ well-calibrated with the observed error in the data?'' The agent then receives a reward if its query made the audit score better than it was in the last step.

The principle behind this RL formulation is that the agent is not trying to create a surrogate that is identical to the true objective, rather it is trying to make its surrogate's uncertainty estimate look good according to a particular yardstick. Different yardsticks reward different querying behavior, so we train 15 agents with 15 different yardsticks to get 15 different ``personalities'' in the committee. The 15 agents share the same network architecture, the same state representation, the same Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm, and 500K training steps. Only the reward function differs.

\subsection{Three benchmark problems}

The same pipeline is run on three problems of increasing input dimension, so we can ask whether any conclusion about committees is a property of the method or an accident of the 1-D case. The second is genuinely multi-objective, and exists so that the RL arm is framed the same way as the MOBO benchmark used elsewhere in this work.

Inputs are normalised to $[0,1]^d$ in every case, so the action space is $\mathrm{Box}(0,1)^d$. Simple regret is computed against $f^\star$, which is exact for both single-objective problems: a literature value for Forrester, and $0$ by construction for colour matching, since the Fr\'echet distance is a metric and the simulator's target RGB is reachable inside the action space.

\paragraph{The multi-objective arm.} In \textsc{Branin--Currin (MO)} the agent fits \emph{two} independent bootstrap surrogates, one per objective, so there are two uncertainty streams to audit rather than one. Each of the 15 audit metrics is evaluated separately on each objective's $(y, \mu, \sigma)$ and the two per-step deltas are \textbf{averaged} into the scalar reward. Averaging rather than summing keeps the reward on the same scale as the single-objective arms, and leaves the one objective-independent metric (MahalanobisOOD, which reads only the geometry of the queried inputs) identical across arms rather than doubled. Each objective is divided by its own scale before auditing --- $50.0$ for Branin, $2.6$ for Currin --- which is not cosmetic: under a shared scale Currin's standard deviation is $0.05$ against Branin's $1.26$, its reward deltas are some $25\times$ weaker, and the agents learn to ignore it entirely.

Because both objectives are audited, the state carries a mean grid, an uncertainty grid and a set of running summary statistics per objective, giving 932 dimensions.

\subsection{Sampling the objective as a sequential decision problem}

To train an RL agent we formulate ``query the objective 100 times'' as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), a tuple $(S, A, P, R, \gamma)$. Each feature maps to a concrete object in this experiment.

The state $S$ is a fixed-dimensional summary of everything the agent sees: the surrogate's predicted mean $\mu$ on a grid over $[0,1]^d$, its predicted $\sigma$ on the same grid, the normalized step count $t/100$, the running mean and variance of observed $y$, the current best $y$, and a query-density histogram. With $g$ grid points and $b$ histogram bins \emph{per axis}, and $m$ objectives (so that $\mu$, $\sigma$ and the three running summaries are stored once per objective),
\[
\dim(S) = 2mg^{d} + 1 + 3m + b^{d}.
\]
For $m = 1$ this is the familiar $2g^d + 4 + b^d$.
The action $A$ is a point $x \in [0,1]^d$, and the agent's policy $\pi(x \mid s)$ maps a state to a distribution over query points. The transition $P$ takes $(s_t, x_t)$, samples $y_t = f(x_t) + \varepsilon_t$ from the noisy oracle, appends $(x_t, y_t)$ to the dataset, refits the surrogate, and recomputes the state summary. Transitions are stochastic because the oracle is noisy and the surrogate is a bootstrap.

The per-axis grid resolution falls sharply with dimension. This is forced: keeping 200 points per axis would make $\mu$ and $\sigma$ alone an 80{,}000-dimensional state per objective on Branin--Currin and a 16-million-dimensional state for colour. It is worth being precise about what this coarsening does and does not affect. It limits only what the agent \emph{observes}. The oracle is continuous and is evaluated at the exact real-valued query; the surrogate is a closed-form polynomial that can be evaluated anywhere; and the action space is continuous and is never snapped to the grid. The grid is a sensor resolution, not a discretisation of the problem. Measured against the true surface, linear interpolation from the state grid is accurate to a mean of $0.34\%$ of range on Branin--Currin and $0.68\%$ on colour, with worst cases of $1.5\%$ and $9.5\%$ respectively.

\subsection{Surrogate and its uncertainty}

The surrogate is a bootstrap ensemble of ridge-regression polynomials: 30 fits, each on a bootstrap resample of the data seen so far, with monomial features of total degree $\le 5$ (Forrester, Branin--Currin) or $\le 3$ (colour). The predicted mean is the ensemble mean and \textbf{$\sigma(x)$ is the standard deviation across the 30 fits}. There is no analytic variance and no Gaussian process; $\sigma$ is purely the ensemble's disagreement, which grows where data does not pin the polynomial down --- fitting on $x \in [0, 0.6]$ only, $\sigma$ rises from $0.067$ at $x = 0.4$ to $7.27$ at $x = 1.0$.

Two properties of this choice matter for interpreting the results. First, $\sigma$ is \emph{epistemic}: it carries no explicit noise term, and tripling the oracle's observation noise moves the mean $\sigma$ only from $0.37$ to $0.50$. Several audit metrics (NLL, IntervalScore, ConformalCoverage) implicitly assume $\sigma$ covers observation noise, and it does not. Second, $\sigma$ inherits the polynomial basis's blind spots: where a degree-5 polynomial simply cannot express the surface, all 30 fits are wrong in the same way, agree with each other, and report small $\sigma$ alongside large error. Fitting on 2000 points, the best achievable $R^2$ is $0.925$ (Forrester), $0.987$ (Branin) and $0.978$ (colour), so such regions exist on every problem.

\subsection{The 15 reward functions}

The reward $R$ is the only thing that differs between the 15 agents during training and is the sole influence on their ``personalities''. For the twelve calibration-style agents, the reward at step $t$ is
\[
r_t = \text{sign} \cdot [\text{check}(t+1) - \text{check}(t)],
\]
i.e.\ how much the audit improved this step, with $\text{sign} = -1$ for losses to minimise and $+1$ for goodness-of-fit measures. The three signal-based agents (UncertaintyEvolution, UncertaintyAnomaly, MahalanobisOOD) score the trailing $\sigma$ or query-location series rather than a $(y, \mu, \sigma)$ triple. Rewards are per-agent z-score normalized so SAC sees a roughly unit-scale signal. An episode is 100 queries, the SAC discount is $\gamma = 0.99$, and each episode starts with 20 random warm-start points drawn from a fresh oracle. We use SAC with a separate actor, critic, and replay buffer per agent, 500K training steps, and 5 seeds, with the same hyperparameters across all 15 agents.

\subsection{Scoring policies with simple regret}

When minimizing $f$, \textbf{simple regret} at the end of a run is the gap between the best value found and the true minimum of $f$, evaluated on the \emph{clean} objective so a lucky noise draw cannot push it below zero. For each policy we run 20 random episode seeds, paired across all policies, and take the mean. That single number is the headline score for that committee member.

On the multi-objective arm simple regret is unavailable, and would be misleading if forced: a policy that drove Branin to its minimum while never moving Currin would score perfectly. We use the \textbf{log hypervolume shortfall} instead,
\[
  \log_{10}\bigl(HV_{\max} - HV_t\bigr),
\]
where $HV_t$ is the hypervolume dominated by the clean objective values of all points queried up to step $t$, measured against the reference point $(18, 6)$, and $HV_{\max} = 56.956$ is the value attained by a dense $120\times120$ grid. Lower is better and the quantity decreases monotonically, exactly as simple regret does, so the same paired-Wilcoxon machinery applies without modification. It is likewise computed on the clean objectives: scoring on noisy draws could inflate the hypervolume, which is the multi-objective analogue of a negative simple regret. Because the two scores are on different scales, the multi-objective column is never compared numerically against the other two --- only the \emph{ranking} of policies within it is.

\section{Results and Discussion}
% ===========================================================================

The main text reports the \textsc{Forrester} problem in full. The colour-matching and \textsc{Branin--Currin (MO)} arms follow the same protocol and are reported in the Supplementary Information (\S\ref{si:colour}--\S\ref{si:mo}); every headline claim below is stated for all three problems, with the supporting figures for the other two in the SI.

\subsection{Learning curves}

Figure \ref{fig:learning_curves} shows smoothed per-agent training reward over the 500K-step run, averaged across seeds, with shaded band indicating the seed spread. The agents split into families by curve shape. CRPS, NLL, IntervalScore, and the calibration-error family all rise sharply in the first $\sim 100$K steps and then decline gradually. The early rewards are gained quickly because any sensible query improves the surrogate from a cold start, but later in training the agent's exploit-low-$\sigma$ policy starts repeating itself, leading to anti-exploratory behavior. ConformalCoverage and IntervalCoverage learn steadily with no late decline. PITUniformity, VarianceAlignment, and VarErrCorrelation all hover near a fixed level with high variance; their rewards are $p$-values, ratios, or rank correlations, which barely move per step, so SAC receives a near-zero gradient most of the time.

The observation that survives from the 9-metric version of this experiment is that \emph{training stability and downstream performance are not the same property}. The agents with the noisiest reward signals are not the worst performers, and the agents that train most cleanly are not the best.

\subsection{Query densities}

Figure \ref{fig:density_forrester} shows where each agent prefers to query on Forrester, one panel per agent: a histogram over $[0,1]$ with the objective overlaid. Tall narrow peaks indicate an agent that has learned to focus on one region, and flat broad shapes mean it explores. The agents whose reward pushes them to exploit low-$\sigma$ regions concentrate on the left half of the interval where noise is small but the true optimum is not; the agents whose reward pushes them to cover quantile gaps or align $\sigma$ with error spread further right, into the noisy half where the true minimum lives. The three signal-based agents (UncertaintyEvolution, UncertaintyAnomaly, MahalanobisOOD) are the ones that put the most mass in the right half, and they are also the best-performing solos.

\subsection{Reward correlation between agents}

A correlation matrix quantifies how similar agent $i$'s per-step reward stream is to agent $j$'s, from $-1$ (perfectly opposite) through $0$ (unrelated) to $+1$ (identical). Both matrices in Figure \ref{fig:corr_pair} are computed the same way: roll out a policy, log the per-step reward for each of the 15 audit metrics, then compute the Pearson correlation of those 15 reward streams across all steps. The trained matrix pools traces from every agent and every seed; the random matrix samples $x$ uniformly and runs 50 episodes of that random policy.

The central observation is unchanged from the 9-metric experiment and in fact strengthened: \textbf{similar structure appears in the trained and random matrices, so that structure cannot have come from learning}. It comes from the metrics themselves, several of which are mathematically related. The most extreme case is CalibrationError $\leftrightarrow$ KuleshovCalibration, which correlate at $+1.00$ in both the trained \emph{and} random matrices on \emph{all three problems} --- these two metrics are effectively redundant as reward signals. Hierarchical clustering at a $1 - |\rho|$ threshold of $0.5$ finds 9--11 clusters among the 15 metrics depending on problem and policy, so the 15 yardsticks are not 15 independent axes of behaviour. The random control shows that training amplifies tendencies already present in the metric definitions rather than creating personalities from nothing.

\subsection{Terminal score across solo agents and the uniform committee}

Table \ref{tab:solo} ranks the solo policies on all three problems. Three things stand out.

First, \textbf{the best solo agent is problem-dependent}. It is a signal-based agent on both single-objective problems --- MahalanobisOOD on Forrester, UncertaintyEvolution on colour --- but \emph{not} on the multi-objective arm, where ConformalCoverage and CalibrationError take the top two places and are effectively tied ($0.980$ vs $0.982$), with UncertaintyAnomaly third. PITUniformity, which was the best agent in the 9-metric version of this experiment, is now only second on Forrester. Any claim about which audit metric makes a good acquisition reward is therefore not portable between problems, and adding a second objective is enough to break it.

Second, \textbf{the uniform-pick committee is never significantly better than the best solo}. It is significantly worse on Forrester ($0.119$ vs $0.0052$, $p = 6.3\times10^{-5}$) and colour ($13354$ vs $10614$, $p = 2.5\times10^{-3}$). The multi-objective arm is the single case where the committee is nominally \emph{ahead} ($0.9645$ vs $0.9804$) --- but the margin is well inside the noise ($p = 0.93$), so the honest reading is a tie, not a win.

Third, the multi-objective arm compresses the field. Its best and worst solo agents differ by a factor of $1.7$ in the log-shortfall ($0.980$ to $1.681$), where Forrester's differ by a factor of $134$. With two objectives to satisfy, no single metric's preferred querying style is as decisive as it is on one. On colour the opposite degeneracy appears: five agents tie at exactly $13878$, the value reached by a policy that never improves on its warm start, which indicates saturation rather than a meaningful ranking among those five.

\subsection{Aggregation rules over the committee}

Rather than rotating uniformly through the 15 members, the votes can be combined more intelligently. We compare: \texttt{agree} (average all 15 preferred actions), \texttt{disagree} (query the point the committee is most divided about), \texttt{weighted-indep} (weight by behavioural independence, from the correlation matrix), and \texttt{weighted-invreg} (weight by $1/$solo regret). In $d$ dimensions the committee's preferred actions are vectors, \texttt{agree} takes the centroid, and \texttt{disagree} maximises the trace of the locally weighted covariance, which reduces exactly to the 1-D weighted variance.

\textbf{No aggregation rule significantly beats the best solo agent on any of the three problems.} This is the headline result, and it is a reversal of the 9-metric finding. In that earlier experiment \texttt{weighted-invreg} beat best-solo by an order of magnitude; with the full 15-metric registry it is $3.5\times$ worse than best-solo on Forrester ($p = 0.019$) and dramatically worse on the other two. The earlier result was an artifact of a small metric set, in which inverse-regret weighting collapsed onto ``use PITUniformity, with the others as tiebreakers.''

Two further observations. \texttt{agree} and \texttt{weighted-indep} remain catastrophic on all three problems, and for the reasons previously identified: averaging disagreeing members lands in a region none of them wanted, a failure that worsens with dimension because a centroid in $d$ dimensions can be further from every member than it can on a line; and ``most independent'' is not ``most useful'' --- the most independent agent by correlation is MahalanobisOOD on Forrester and colour, whose colour regret is poor. On the multi-objective arm these two, together with \texttt{disagree} and \texttt{weighted-invreg}, converge on essentially the same poor value ($1.677$--$1.681$, all $p \approx 2\times10^{-6}$), matching the worst solo agent exactly. They have collapsed onto a degenerate policy.

Most uncomfortably, \texttt{random} is statistically indistinguishable from the best solo on colour ($p = 0.47$), and is nominally the best entry in that column.

\textbf{The multi-objective arm is the one place this does not happen}, and it is the most useful thing the arm contributes. There \texttt{random} is decisively \emph{beaten} by the best solo ($1.406$ vs $0.980$, $p = 1.3\times10^{-5}$), and the uniform committee is the best entry in the column. Hypervolume requires a policy to make progress on both objectives, and a single lucky query cannot deliver that the way it can deliver low simple regret. This supports the reading that random search's apparent competitiveness on colour is substantially an artifact of what simple regret measures, rather than evidence that the trained agents are worthless: given a score that cannot be won by one lucky draw, the trained agents separate from random cleanly.

\section{Conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

Reward-shaping with uncertainty-audit metrics does produce stylistically distinct agents, but on roughly 9--11 independent axes rather than 15, and the random-control matrices show that most of that structure is intrinsic to the metric definitions rather than learned. Two of the fifteen metrics (CalibrationError and KuleshovCalibration) are effectively identical as reward signals, correlating at $+1.00$ under both trained and random policies on every problem.

The central negative result is that \textbf{no committee aggregation scheme we tested significantly beats the best individual agent on any of the three problems}, and on colour a uniformly random acquisition policy is statistically indistinguishable from the best agent. The one scheme that appeared to win in a smaller 9-metric version of this experiment, inverse-regret weighting, does not survive the expansion to 15 metrics; it was an artifact of a committee in which a single member dominated. Averaging members' picks and weighting by behavioural independence remain reliably catastrophic.

The multi-objective arm qualifies that second clause in a way worth carrying forward. When the score is hypervolume rather than simple regret --- so that no single lucky query can win, and progress on both objectives is required --- random search is beaten decisively ($p = 1.3\times10^{-5}$) and the uniform committee is nominally the best policy tested, though its margin over the best solo agent is not significant. The trained agents are therefore not indistinguishable from chance in general; they are indistinguishable from chance \emph{under a score that a single lucky query can win}. This does not rescue the committee hypothesis, but it does relocate part of the negative result from the agents to the metric used to judge them.

The most interesting finding is negative in a more specific way. On the colour-matching problem the audited objective is essentially a function of one input channel, and the surrogate's calibration is \emph{best} in precisely the region where the design objective is \emph{worst}. Twelve of fifteen agents duly spend almost their entire budget there. The agents are not failing to optimise; they are optimising exactly what they were paid to optimise, and what they were paid to optimise is anti-correlated with the goal. The three agents that resist this --- all three of the signal-based rewards, which score the trajectory of uncertainty rather than its instantaneous calibration --- are the three best performers on that problem, and signal-based agents are the best solo on both single-objective problems. On the multi-objective arm they are not: ConformalCoverage and CalibrationError lead there, with UncertaintyAnomaly third, so this conclusion is the one that a second objective most clearly disturbs.

This suggests the practical conclusion is not about committees at all. It is that \emph{instantaneous calibration quality is a poor acquisition signal}, because a surrogate can be well-calibrated and uninformative at the same time, while metrics that score how uncertainty \emph{evolves} retain some connection to whether the campaign is making progress.

\subsection*{Caveats}

Several limitations bear on how far these results generalise.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Surrogate capacity.} All results are conditioned on a bootstrap polynomial surrogate whose best achievable $R^2$ is $0.93$--$0.99$. Its $\sigma$ is purely epistemic and carries no noise model, which several of the audit metrics implicitly assume it does.
\item \textbf{Acquisition-grid resolution.} The grid-search baselines search 300 points in 1-D but only 225 (Branin--Currin) and 512 (colour) candidates in higher dimensions, since a 300-per-axis grid is infeasible. Cross-problem comparisons of LCB and max-$\sigma$ are therefore not strictly like-for-like.
\item \textbf{Simple regret rewards a single lucky query.} It asks only how low the best value seen so far is, never where or how reliably that value was found, which inflates the apparent competitiveness of random search --- as the multi-objective arm, scored by hypervolume, demonstrates directly.
\item \textbf{The audit metrics remain per-objective.} On the multi-objective arm each of the 15 checks is applied to each objective's surrogate separately and the results averaged. No metric inspects the \emph{joint} residual structure of the two surrogates, and none is aware of Pareto dominance. The arm therefore answers ``do these metrics, applied to a two-surrogate model, help or harm hypervolume?'' It does not evaluate any multi-objective-aware audit metric, and nothing here should be read as a claim about one.
\item \textbf{Scalarisation in the baselines.} The LCB and max-$\sigma$ baselines on the multi-objective arm reduce the two objectives by an unweighted sum. This is a deliberate simplification rather than a tuned choice, and it is part of why both rules perform poorly there; a proper multi-objective acquisition function (e.g.\ expected hypervolume improvement) would be a fairer baseline.
\item \textbf{Budget.} 100 acquisition steps after a 20-point warm start is short for a 3-D problem; the colour saturation at $13878$ reflects policies that never improved on their warm start at all.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{The colour problem exposes explicit reward hacking}
\label{sec:hacking}

The chromaticity projection in Figure \ref{fig:density_color} is the natural way to display colour, but it divides out intensity: the entire $R = G = B$ diagonal of the RGB cube collapses onto the single D65 point, and a policy pinned against a channel's upper bound is invisible. Figure \ref{fig:channels} plots the raw per-channel marginals instead, and the picture is stark. \textbf{Twelve of the fifteen agents spend 75--100\% of their queries at $B > 0.95$}, against the target's $B = 0.348$.

This is not incidental, and it is not a failure of optimisation. The audited objective is very nearly a function of $B$ alone: Spearman $\rho$ with Fr\'echet distance is $+0.83$ for $B$, $+0.03$ for $R$ and $-0.12$ for $G$. Sweeping $B$ from $0.5$ to $1.0$ moves the objective from $31{,}644$ to $128{,}834$, while sweeping $R$ across its entire range moves it by $51$, about $0.16\%$. High $B$ is therefore the \emph{worst} region of the space for the true objective. But it is the \emph{best} region for the audit: the surrogate's standardised residual $|y - \mu|/\sigma$ falls from $1.42$ in the low-$B$ band to $0.69$ in the high-$B$ band, because the polynomial is smoothly wrong there and all 30 bootstrap fits agree with each other about it.

The calibration-style rewards therefore pay the agents to sit exactly where the true objective is worst, and the agents comply. The three agents that do \emph{not} saturate $B$ --- UncertaintyEvolution ($6\%$), MahalanobisOOD ($20\%$), UncertaintyAnomaly ($53\%$) --- are precisely the three best solos on this problem. This is the cleanest demonstration in the study that an audit metric and a design objective can be actively anti-correlated.

\subsection*{SI-3: Committee votes as features in the AL state space}
\label{si:votes}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

A different way to use the committee is to treat its votes as \emph{features} for a classical Bayesian-optimization rule rather than as a policy in their own right. At each step we ask the 15 agents where they would query, and add a soft penalty on distance from their preferred points to the acquisition score. We test two base rules: LCB, which balances exploration and exploitation by minimising $\mu(x) - \kappa\sigma(x)$, and max-$\sigma$, which is pure exploration.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & Forrester & Colour & Branin--Currin (MO) \\
\midrule
LCB                 & 0.0532 & 11860 & 1.604 \\
LCB + votes         & 0.0561 & 11874 & 1.610 \\
$p$                 & 0.88   & 0.50 & 0.50 \\
\midrule
max-$\sigma$        & 0.135  & 12149 & 1.657 \\
max-$\sigma$ + votes & \textbf{0.0082} & 13774 & 1.652 \\
$p$                 & \textbf{0.003} & \textbf{0.001} & 0.68 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Votes-as-features, mean terminal score over 20 paired seeds. Bold marks a significant change. The first two columns are simple regret; the third is the log hypervolume shortfall.}
\label{tab:votes}
\end{table}

The 9-metric experiment concluded that committee votes rescue a deliberately under-informed rule (max-$\sigma$) while adding nothing to an already-strong one (LCB). \textbf{That conclusion does not generalise.} It holds on Forrester, where max-$\sigma$ improves by a factor of 16 ($p = 0.003$) and LCB is unmoved. On colour the votes are actively \emph{harmful} to max-$\sigma$ ($12149 \to 13774$, $p = 0.001$): the vote-augmented policy makes no progress past its warm start on 16 of 20 seeds, because the committee's preferred points cluster tightly (the $B\!\approx\!1$ region of \S\ref{sec:hacking}) and the distance penalty pins acquisition there regardless of $\sigma$.

On the multi-objective arm the votes do \emph{nothing at all}: both base rules move by less than $0.006$ in the log shortfall, neither approaching significance ($p = 0.50$ and $p = 0.68$). This is the cleanest null of the three, and it is informative because both base rules are poor here in absolute terms --- at $1.60$ and $1.66$ they sit among the worst policies in Table \ref{tab:agg}, below even \texttt{random}. The grid-search baselines scalarise the two objectives by summing them, which is a crude compromise on a problem where the objectives conflict, and no amount of committee-vote steering repairs a rule that is optimising the wrong scalar.

The honest summary is that committee votes are a prior over where to look, and whether that prior helps depends on whether it happens to point somewhere useful on the problem at hand. It is not a property of the base rule's sophistication.
